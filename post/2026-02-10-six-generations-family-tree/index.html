Last week, I found an old, crumpled piece of paper in my grandmother’s house. It was a hand-drawn family tree, barely legible, with names and dates scribbled in fading ink. That fragile document, torn and worn by time, contained six generations of my family’s history — 44 people whose lives intersected to create mine.

I decided to digitize it, not just to preserve the information, but to truly understand the web of connections that brought me here.

The Full Picture

This isn’t just my direct lineage. This is the complete family — siblings, cousins, aunts, uncles, half-siblings, second marriages. The messy, beautiful reality of how families actually work.

The Stories

Generation 2: The Three Brothers

Manuel Pereira Tender and Inês had three sons:

  1. António Pereira Tender - The eldest
  2. Guilherme Augusto Pereira (“Tio Capitão”) - The middle brother
  3. Hermínio Albino Pereira (1884-1934) - My great-grandfather, died at 50

I know nothing about António and Guilherme. What did they do? Did they have children? Those branches are lost to time.

Generation 3: The Immortals

Hermínio and Júlia had 6 children. Five lived to extraordinary ages:

  • António Joaquim (1911-1956) - Died young at 45
  • Belmira (1916-2012) - 96 years
  • Inês (1918-2015) - 96 years
  • Aurea (1919-2015) - 96 years
  • Hermínia (1927-2018) - 91 years
  • Maria Ester (1934-present) - Still alive at 90

What gene did they carry? Four siblings hitting 90+, three reaching 96? That’s not luck—that’s something in the blood.

The Half-Siblings Nobody Talks About

Adelino da Costa (my grandfather) had three half-siblings from his father’s other relationship:

  • Albino da Costa (1915-2002) - Died with Alzheimer’s at 84
  • Isaura da Costa - Had special needs
  • Maria Teresa (b. 1926) - Had 13 children, 8 survived

These are the family members that exist in whispers. I never met them. But they’re part of the story.

Generation 5: The Cousins

My generation has 10 people:

My immediate family:

  • Me (Paulo) and my sister Sílvia

Aunt Raquel’s kids:

  • Victor (25-04-1983)
  • Jorge (21-10-1985) - who gave Matild her cousins Helena & Miguel

Uncle Hermínio’s kids:

  • Sandra - mother of Guilherme
  • Sónia - mother of Mafalda (divorced)

We grew up together. Summer holidays, Sunday lunches, birthday parties. Now we’re scattered — UK, Portugal, different lives. But we’re bound by this tree.

Generation 6: The Future

Six children carry this forward:

  • Matild (8) and Nelson (10 months) - my kids
  • Helena (~9) and Miguel (~6) - Jorge’s kids
  • Guilherme and Mafalda - Sandra and Sónia’s kids

They’ll grow up knowing (or not knowing) this history. They’ll decide what to keep and what to forget.

What I Learned

  1. Families aren’t linear - They’re messy. Half-siblings, divorces, second marriages, children with special needs, early deaths. That’s reality.

  2. Longevity clusters - If one sibling lives to 96, check the others. Genes matter.

  3. Some branches die - António and Guilherme Pereira had no (known) descendants. Their line ended.

  4. Migration scatters families - Generation 5 is spread across continents. Generation 6 will barely know each other.

  5. 44 lives led to mine - Every single person on this tree made choices (consciously or not) that resulted in my existence. That’s humbling.

The Gaps

I still don’t know:

  • What António and Guilherme Pereira did
  • Whether Refina (Júlia’s sister) had children
  • The names of Sandra and Sónia’s partners
  • What happened to most of António Matias da Costa’s descendants
  • Maria Teresa’s 13 children (8 survived)—who were they?

Family trees have holes. Always.

To My Children

Matild and Nelson,

You see those six kids at the bottom? That’s your generation. You have four cousins (that I know of): Helena, Miguel, Guilherme, Mafalda.

You’re all connected to:

  • Manuel Pereira Tender (your great-great-great-grandfather)
  • Hermínio Albino Pereira (your great-great-grandfather)
  • The “Immortal Siblings” who lived to 96
  • A family that moved from rural Portugal to cities to countries

Your grandmother Maria Ester (my avó) is still alive. You’ve met her. Remember that. Most kids don’t get to know their great-great-grandmother.

When you have children (if you do), show them this tree. Tell them where they came from.
